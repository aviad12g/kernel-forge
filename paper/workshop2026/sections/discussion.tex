\section{Positioning, limitations, and conclusion}

KernelBench measures generated-kernel capability at scale~\citep{kernelbench2025};
KernelBench-Verified adds hidden distributions, realistic precision settings,
and memory analysis~\citep{zhang2026kernelbenchverified}; robust-kbench broadens
verification scenarios~\citep{lange2025robustkbench}; and FastKernels studies
production alignment~\citep{oliaro2026fastkernels}. Harness Engineering archives
workload-grounded optimization evidence~\citep{shui2026harness}. \system{} does
not compete on generation capability. Its narrower focus is the statistical and
lifecycle validity of promoting one measured winner after candidate search.
Contract-Grade Verifier formalizes structural and behavioral kernel
contracts~\citep{shah2026contractgrade}, while Correct but Slow shows that weak
verification can admit semantically deficient kernels that appear
fast~\citep{li2026correctslow}. Our verifier adopts the directly enforceable
output-tree, alias, mutation, special-value, and repeated-execution checks, while
leaving task-specific distribution and precision oracles explicit rather than
claiming generic coverage.

The external study is limited to one Tesla T4, a deterministic feasible subset
rather than a random sample of KernelBench L1, one model, and three candidates
per task. The fused8 studies cover four tasks in one shape regime. The
near-threshold stress test and an easy-grid hardware control ran on one RTX
A4500. Its four-task bootstrap interval is
wide, and calibration intentionally enriches near-threshold candidates rather
than estimating their natural frequency. The 38/48 invalid-task count and the
fact that every valid task is matrix multiplication show that candidate validity
and family structure dominate this campaign. Seven processes across two time
periods provide a practical holdout check but not precise tail modeling. The
fixed 2\% promotion margin is a
prespecified practical threshold, not a noise-calibrated equivalence bound.
Candidate generation is model-specific,
whereas both multiplicity regimes use deterministic templates; the studies
answer different questions. CUDA graphs, multi-GPU replication, deployment-cost
amortization, and benchmark-wide model comparisons are outside scope.
Compiler-relative evidence is also limited: the baseline was available for
47/48 selected tasks and all 10 valid-task winners at screening, while only one
frozen winner received separate fresh-process compiler confirmation.

Generated-kernel systems need more than plausible source and one fast timing
sample. The completed protocol makes screening, confirmation, and evaluator
validity separately auditable. Here it changes the defensible conclusion: the
corrected external campaign supports no above-eager kernel claim, the easy grid
bounds multiplicity concerns, the calibrated stress test exposes a screen-only
promotion, and the lifecycle ablation isolates a host-timing confound.
Independent re-evaluation is therefore a useful default for generated-kernel
claims, even when its result is a bound or a null finding rather than a new
speedup.
